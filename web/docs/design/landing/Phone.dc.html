How it works

  1. 1

    Pick a spot

    Type a place in the sky or a name. Or start with a famous object, like Barnard's Star.

  2. 2

    Flip

    Hold one key. The picture switches between two dates, months apart.

  3. 3

    See what jumps

    Whatever stayed put looks frozen. Anything that moved or changed catches your eye.

The same trick found Pluto

Earlier

1930

Clyde Tombaugh flipped back and forth between two photographs of the same patch of sky, taken six nights apart. One faint dot jumped. It was Pluto.

Later

Now

You flip between two pictures from a space telescope, taken months apart. It is the same trick, and it is where this site gets its name.

Your turn

Now a harder one

This is WISE 0855−0714. It is a brown dwarf, too small to shine like a star, and the coldest one known. It is also one of the Sun's nearest neighbours.

Hold the key and watch the faint dot in the very middle. In six months it slid a little to the west, which is to the right.

What it shows

Nearby stars that move or change from one picture to the next. That is all it claims.

Real pictures

Every image comes from SPHEREx, a NASA space telescope that photographs the whole sky every six months. They look small and coarse because that is what the telescope really sees.
